05 / REFERENCE

Plain-language glossary

A quick guide to the terms above. The model and calculations stay first; use this when you want a simpler explanation.

Kinematics
The geometry of how an arm moves. Here, position is calculated from joint angles and link sizes—not from forces or motor power.
Degree of freedom
One independent way the arm can move. This arm has six rotary joints, so six angle values describe its pose.
Joint & link
A joint is a rotating connection; a link is the rigid segment between joints. The servos turn the joints, and the links carry the motion.
Servo angle (θ)
The commanded turn from a joint’s zero reference, measured here in degrees. A real servo may need its zero and travel calibrated.
Coordinate frame
A local set of X, Y, and Z directions—like a tiny ruler attached to the base or a joint. Frame origins are reported in base coordinates.
D-H parameters
Four values that describe each step between frames: θ turns about Z, d shifts along Z, a shifts along X, and α twists about X. In this model, θ changes as you move a control.
Transform matrix
A 4 × 4 table that stores a frame’s rotation and position together. Multiplying the six joint transforms carries the base frame out to the tool.
Forward kinematics
The calculation that uses the six servo angles and known link dimensions to predict where the tool is and which way it points.
TCP / end effector
The tool-center point is the chosen endpoint of the gripper. The readout gives its position relative to the base, plus its orientation.
RPY (ZYX)
Roll, pitch, and yaw are three angles used to describe orientation. This readout uses the ZYX rotation order shown beside it.
Rigid-link assumption
The model treats every link as perfectly stiff and each joint as exact. Real arms can flex, have backlash, or miss a command slightly.

How the goal point is solved
d = |TCP − S| cos β = (d² + L₁² − Lf²) / 2 d L₁
θ₂ = α ± β   (α = elevation of the goal, β = elbow triangle)
θ₃ = θ₂ − ψ   (ψ = elevation of the goal seen from the elbow)

Yaw points the arm at the goal first, then the shoulder-to-tip line and the elbow form a triangle. Up lifts the elbow above that line (a crane); down folds it underneath. With the tool below the shoulder only the raised solution stays inside the shoulder's travel.

MODEL NOTE Ideal revolute joints, rigid links, no self-collision or floor checking beyond the two planes. The tool stays collinear with the forearm, so the gripper's reach matters and its roll does not.

05 / REFERENCE

Plain-language glossary

Two-axis shoulder
One joint turns the arm around the vertical stand (yaw) and the other tips it up and down (pitch). Two axes at one joint = two degrees of freedom.
Raised elbow
The shoulder throws the upper arm above horizontal, so the elbow sits in the air above the bench the arm is mounted on.
Working plane
One of the two flat surfaces. Plane 1 is where the arm is bolted; plane 2 is the lower bench the tool reaches down to.
Forearm elevation
The angle of the forearm from horizontal. Negative values mean it hangs downward, which is what reaches plane 2.
Elbow up / down
Two mirror solutions for the same goal point: elbow above the shoulder-to-goal line, or below it.
Reach on a plane
The ring of points the tool can touch on a given surface, limited by how far the arm can extend and how far it can fold.
Degrees of freedom
The independent motions available. Yaw, shoulder pitch and elbow pitch set X, Y and Z of the tool — three in, three out.
Jacobian determinant
How freely the tool can move in all three directions. Zero means a stretched or folded pose where some directions are impossible.
